**Hockey Night in Canada CBC** broadcasts have ended. Not the programme — that continues on Sportsnet — but the seventy-four-year arrangement under which any household in the country could watch Saturday night NHL hockey with an antenna, a television and no subscription of any kind. That specific thing is over, and for a particular group of Canadians it is the most expensive change in Canadian television this decade.

![A family comparing television options after Hockey Night in Canada CBC broadcasts ended](https://images.pexels.com/photos/7693548/pexels-photo-7693548.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Quick Answer

The Rogers–CBC sublicensing agreement expired at the end of the **2025-26 Stanley Cup playoffs** and was not renewed for 2026-27, the first year of Rogers' new twelve-year rights deal. Citytv has also stopped carrying national telecasts. **Hockey Night in Canada continues on Sportsnet**, so the show survives — but the free over-the-air path to it does not. For anyone who watched by antenna, hockey went from $0 to at least $34.99 a month.

## Hockey Night in Canada CBC Coverage: What Ended and What Did Not

It is worth being precise, because the headlines have blurred two different things.

| | Status for 2026-27 |
|---|---|
| Hockey Night in Canada, the programme | **Continues** — a Sportsnet property |
| Saturday night national games | Continue on Sportsnet |
| CBC carriage of those games | **Ended** |
| Citytv national telecasts | **Ended** |
| Free over-the-air national NHL | **Ended** |

Under the old arrangement, Sportsnet produced the games, retained editorial control and managed the advertising; CBC provided the over-the-air window. That window is what closed. The production, the branding and the Saturday slot all continue — behind a subscription.

## The Group This Actually Hits

Most coverage of this story has been about nostalgia. The more useful story is about who was relying on the free window, because it was not a small or random group.

The audience that relied on Hockey Night in Canada CBC coverage was not a random slice of the country. Over-the-air television in Canada skews strongly toward **older households, rural and small-town viewers, and lower-income households** — people for whom an antenna was a deliberate financial decision rather than an oversight. For them, the practical effect is not that a tradition ended. It is that a weekly, no-cost source of entertainment now carries a monthly bill roughly equivalent to a phone plan.

There is a second group worth naming: households that already cut cable and kept an antenna specifically as the backstop for live sport and news. That strategy worked well for years and no longer covers hockey.

## Five Changes After the Hockey Night in Canada CBC Exit

**1. Saturday hockey now needs a subscription.** *Fix:* Sportsnet+ monthly, subscribed in October and cancelled in May. Do not take the annual plan unless you watch other Sportsnet content through the summer.

**2. The antenna no longer covers live sport.** *Fix:* keep it anyway for local news and emergency broadcasting — it costs nothing to leave in place — and treat streaming as an addition rather than a replacement.

**3. Wednesday games moved somewhere unexpected.** *Fix:* if the household already pays for Amazon Prime for deliveries, those games are included at no extra cost. Many people are entitled to them and do not realise it.

**4. A streaming setup is more complicated than an antenna.** *Fix:* set it up before opening night, not at puck drop. One device, one input, one app, logged in and tested with a full game. Our [installation guide](/installation-guide) covers the sequence, and the [IPTV for seniors Canada](/blog/iptv-for-seniors-canada) guide is written specifically around keeping the day-to-day operation down to a single remote.

**5. Streams run behind the live event.** *Fix:* expect a delay of roughly five to thirty seconds against radio, the arena and social media. Turning off phone notifications during a game genuinely helps, and it is worth knowing this is normal rather than a fault with your connection.

## Setting Up for Someone Who Has Only Used an Antenna

If you are helping a parent or grandparent through this, the technology is not the hard part — the change in *routine* is. An antenna involved one remote and a channel number. Replacing that well means rebuilding something equally simple, not demonstrating everything a streaming device can do.

**Use one device and one input.** Whatever you choose, set the television to start on it. If the household has to change inputs, the setup has already failed.

**Log in once, permanently.** Enter credentials during setup and enable whatever "stay signed in" option exists. A password prompt at 7 p.m. on a Saturday is the single most common reason a setup gets abandoned.

**Put the app where it opens first.** Most devices allow you to reorder the home screen. Sportsnet should be the first tile, not the eleventh.

**Write the steps on a card.** Three lines, large print, left by the remote: turn on TV, press this button, press this tile. This is not condescending — it is what makes the difference between a setup that lasts and one that gets a phone call every week.

**Do a full dry run together** during a live game before opening night, not with a menu screen. Problems only appear when something is actually playing.

## Cancelling Cleanly, If You Are Changing Providers

If this pushes you to restructure your television entirely, two consumer protections are worth knowing.

Television service in Canada sits under the **Television Service Provider Code** rather than the Wireless or Internet Codes. Under it, a provider **may not require 30 days' notice to cancel**, must give at least **30 days' notice of a price change**, and must set out any early-cancellation formula in the written agreement and the Critical Information Summary.

Note the asymmetry that catches people out: the CRTC's ban on activation, modification and early-cancellation fees, in force since 12 June 2026, applies to the **Wireless and Internet Codes only** — television is not covered by it. So the order in which you cancel services matters. Cancel television first, confirm in writing, and keep the confirmation.

## What Replaces It on CBC

CBC has said it is moving to a new sports programming strategy following the success of the Milan-Cortina Olympic Games, and has signalled that **women's sports will get significant play** in the schedule space that opens up. Whatever fills the Saturday slot, it will be free over the air — which, for the audience that just lost hockey, is the part that matters.

## The Bottom Line

Hockey Night in Canada CBC broadcasts ended; the programme itself did not. What ended was free hockey. It moved fully behind a paywall it had partly sat behind for years, and the households most affected are the ones least able to absorb another monthly bill.

If you watched Saturdays with an antenna, the cheapest honest answer is Sportsnet+ at $34.99 a month for the season, cancelled in the spring. Set it up in September rather than October, and keep the antenna for everything else — it still does its job.
