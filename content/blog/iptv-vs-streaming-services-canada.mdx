IPTV vs streaming services Canada is usually framed as a price fight. It isn't, quite. The two models are organised around **completely different things** — one around titles, the other around channels — and that structural difference predicts which one suits your household far better than the monthly figure does. The price comparison is still worth doing, though, because the streaming stack has quietly become expensive.

## Quick Answer

Four major streaming apps on **ad-supported** plans cost roughly **$47 a month** combined in Canada. Streaming apps are **on-demand libraries** with exclusive catalogues; IPTV delivers **live channels on a schedule**. Apps suit households that watch specific shows. Channel-based services suit households that watch live news, sport, or channels from another country.

## IPTV vs streaming services Canada: the $47 stack

Published Canadian pricing at the time of writing, on base ad-supported tiers:

| Service | Ad-supported | Ad-free |
|---|---|---|
| Netflix | ~$7.99 | up to ~$23.99 |
| Disney+ | ~$8.99 | ~$15.99–$16.99 |
| Crave | ~$19.99 | ~$19.99 |
| Prime Video | ~$9.99 | ~$9.99 + |
| **Combined** | **~$47/month** | **Substantially higher** |

Two observations. First, **$47 a month is roughly $564 a year** — and that is the *cheap* configuration, with advertising. Second, prices in this category move frequently; treat these as indicative and confirm before budgeting.

The reason the stack grows is exclusivity. Each service holds rights to its own catalogue, so a household following four shows on four services genuinely needs four subscriptions. There is no version of the app model where one subscription covers everything.

## The structural difference that actually decides it

| | **Streaming apps** | **Channel-based / IPTV** |
|---|---|---|
| Organised around | **Titles** you choose | **Channels** on a schedule |
| Content | On-demand libraries | Live feeds, plus catch-up |
| Exclusivity | High — one show, one service | Depends on carriage |
| Best for | Specific series and films | Live news, sport, international |
| Discovery | Algorithmic recommendations | Channel surfing / EPG |
| Cost shape | Per service, stacks up | Single subscription |

This is the part most comparisons miss. IPTV vs streaming services Canada is not two versions of the same product at different prices — it is **appointment-free viewing versus scheduled viewing**, and households genuinely differ in which they want.

## IPTV vs streaming services Canada: which fits your household

Deciding IPTV vs streaming services Canada offers takes five minutes: **look at what you actually watched last month**, not what you intend to watch.

**You are better served by streaming apps if:** you watch a handful of specific series, you binge rather than graze, you rarely watch live anything, and you are willing to rotate subscriptions — subscribing for a month, finishing a show, cancelling. Rotation is the single biggest saving available in the app model and very few households do it.

**You are better served by channel-based viewing if:** you leave live news on, you follow sport where the schedule matters, you want channels in a language other than English or French, or your household prefers browsing a guide to searching a library.

**Many households genuinely want both**, which is the honest answer and the reason the $47 stack keeps growing. In that case the question becomes which apps you can drop rather than whether to drop all of them.

## The rights question, stated plainly

One caution that matters more than price.

In Canada, a given show generally exists on **exactly one** legitimate service, because exclusivity is what these companies pay for. Live channels are governed separately — the CRTC maintains a list of authorised non-Canadian services, and licensed distributors may only carry what is on it.

So any service claiming to carry **every** major platform's exclusive content, plus all live channels, is describing something that legitimate licensing does not permit. That does not make every channel-based service suspect — plenty operate with proper rights — but it does mean the rights position is worth checking before you pay. ## What the industry numbers suggest

Two data points from the most recent Canadian earnings season put the shift in context.

**Crave subscriptions rose 23% to 5.07 million** in BCE's Q2 2026 — strong growth for a domestic streaming service. Meanwhile **Rogers shed 111,000 TV subscribers** in the first half of 2026, **TELUS discontinued TV subscriber reporting** entirely from 1 January 2026, and **BCE redefined its video subscriber metric** on the same date to include bundled streaming customers.

The pattern is consistent: **traditional TV subscriptions are shrinking, streaming is growing, and the industry is finding it increasingly awkward to describe the difference.** For households, that means the useful comparison is no longer "TV versus streaming" but which combination of subscriptions matches what you watch.

## The rotation math nobody runs

Rotation is the single largest saving available in the app model, and it is worth showing rather than asserting.

Take a household subscribed to all four services year-round at ad-supported rates:

| Approach | Annual cost | What you give up |
|---|---|---|
| **All four, all year** | **~$564** | Nothing |
| Two at a time, rotating quarterly | **~$280** | Waiting for some releases |
| One at a time, rotating monthly | **~$150** | Watching in blocks, not simultaneously |
| Keep one year-round + rotate one | **~$300** | Very little in practice |

The last row is where most households land comfortably: hold the service you genuinely use weekly, rotate the second slot through the others as things you want to watch appear.

That is roughly **$264 a year** saved for an inconvenience most people describe as trivial once they try it — because streaming libraries are not perishable. A series that came out in March is still there in September.

Where this logic breaks down is **live content**. You cannot rotate into last night's game or this morning's news; if you missed it, it is gone. That asymmetry is the real dividing line in IPTV vs streaming services Canada households weigh: **on-demand content rewards patience, live content punishes it.** A household that watches live sport or news cannot rotate its way to savings the way a drama-watching household can.

## Five ways to cut the stack without losing much

1. **Rotate, don't stack.** Subscribe, watch the season, cancel. Most services make this easy and most households never do it.
2. **Accept advertising on the service you watch least.** The gap between ad and ad-free tiers is often $8 or more.
3. **Check annual plans** on services you keep year-round — Disney+ annual pricing is meaningfully below twelve monthly payments.
4. **Audit for the forgotten subscription.** Almost every household has one it stopped using months ago.
5. **Count live viewing honestly.** If you never watch live, you are paying for channel breadth you don't use — and the reverse is equally true.

## What each model handles badly

Balanced comparisons are more useful than advocacy, so here are the genuine weaknesses on both sides.

**Where streaming apps fall down:**

- **Fragmentation.** Four subscriptions, four interfaces, four watchlists, and no single search across them. The cost of switching between them is attention rather than money.
- **Content disappears.** Licensing expires. A series available in January can be gone in June, and there is no warning.
- **Live is an afterthought.** Sport and news exist on some apps but are rarely the core product, and rights are split unpredictably by region.
- **Price drift.** Every major service has raised prices repeatedly. A stack assembled two years ago costs meaningfully more today for the same content.

**Where channel-based services fall down:**

- **You watch on someone else's schedule.** Catch-up windows help, but a channel model is inherently less flexible than an on-demand library.
- **Quality varies by feed**, not by service. One channel may be excellent and the next poor within the same subscription.
- **Rights positions vary enormously**, and verifying them is the subscriber's responsibility. This is the single most important check in the category.
- **Discovery is weaker.** No algorithm surfaces something you would have liked; you find things by browsing a guide.

Weighed honestly, IPTV vs streaming services Canada is not a contest with a winner. It is a question of which weakness you mind less — **paying repeatedly for fragmented libraries, or watching on a schedule you do not set.** Households that answer that question deliberately spend less and are happier with what they get than households that simply accumulate subscriptions.

## The Bottom Line

IPTV vs streaming services Canada comes down to a question the price tables never ask: **do you watch titles, or do you watch channels?**

If it is titles, streaming apps are built for you — and the discipline that saves real money is rotation, not choosing the cheapest service. If it is channels, especially live sport, news or content in another language, a channel-based service matches your habits in a way no library of on-demand shows will.

The $47 stack is what happens when a household drifts into paying for both without deciding. Spend five minutes reviewing what you actually watched last month; for most people that single review is worth more than any comparison of monthly prices.
