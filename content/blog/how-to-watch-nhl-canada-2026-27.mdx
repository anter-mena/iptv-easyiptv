If you are working out **how to watch NHL in Canada** this season and your old setup no longer covers everything, you have not missed a setting. The map genuinely changed. The 2026-27 season is the first year of Rogers' new twelve-year rights deal, and three separate shifts land at once — one of which closes the free over-the-air route to national hockey for the first time in seven decades.

## Quick Answer

From **30 September 2026**, Prime Video holds exclusive Canadian rights to Wednesday night national games — at least **26 regular season games**, plus **two first-round and one second-round playoff series** a year, in English and French, included with Prime. **Sportsnet keeps more than 500 national games**, including Saturdays and Mondays and most of the playoffs. **CBC is out**, its sublicence having expired after the 2025-26 playoffs, and **Citytv no longer carries national telecasts**.

The important structural point, and the thing that makes how to watch NHL in Canada harder than it used to be: these are not competing packages offering the same games at different prices. They are **mutually exclusive slices of one schedule**. There is no service that carries all of it, and there is no overlap to arbitrage. Whether you need one, two or three depends entirely on which team you follow and how completist you are.

## What Prime Video's Exclusivity Actually Means

Under the twelve-year sublicensing agreement announced by Rogers and Amazon, Prime Video becomes the long-term Canadian home of Wednesday Night Hockey beginning 30 September 2026. It carries at least 26 national regular season games each year in both English and French, plus two first-round playoff series and one second-round series annually.

"Exclusive" is doing real work in that sentence. These games are **not simulcast on Sportsnet or anywhere else**. If you do not have Prime, there is no legal way to watch them — and the playoff series matter more than the regular season count suggests, because a first-round series can run seven games.

The upside is that Prime is the cheapest of the three at $9.99 a month, and the hockey is included at no additional charge for members. A household already paying for Prime for deliveries gains a slice of the NHL schedule at no extra cost, which is genuinely new.

## How to Watch NHL in Canada: Seven Steps for the Season

1. **Start from your team, not the services.** Write down which broadcaster carries your club's regional games. For many fans that single answer removes one of the three subscriptions immediately.
2. **Decide whether you need Wednesdays.** If you watch every game, you need Prime. If you follow one team casually, 26 national games may not include many of theirs.
3. **Buy monthly, not annually.** The season runs October to April, plus playoffs. Twelve-month commitments pay for months with no hockey; annual plans only win if you genuinely watch year-round.
4. **Stack, then cancel.** Subscribe to Prime for the playoff rounds it carries and cancel afterwards. Nothing obliges you to hold all three all season.
5. **Check what your existing TV package already includes.** Sportsnet and TSN linear channels are frequently bundled, which makes a separate streaming subscription redundant.
6. **Wire the main TV.** Live sport punishes inconsistency far more than it punishes speed. An Ethernet cable to your primary screen removes the single largest cause of stutter during a game.
7. **Test before opening night.** Run a full game's worth of playback in the week before 30 September rather than discovering an app or login problem at puck drop.

## Regional Rights Still Apply

One thing that has not changed, and still causes more confusion than anything else: national and regional rights are separate, and a national subscription does not always show you your local team.

Regional rights mean a club's games in its own market are frequently carried by a different broadcaster than the same game elsewhere in the country. A Vancouver viewer and a Halifax viewer can open the same app on the same night and see different things — that is the system working as designed, not a fault.

Two practical consequences. **Check your team's regional carrier before subscribing to anything**, because it may be the one service you actually need and the national package may be the redundant purchase. And **expect east and west feeds to differ** on the same channel number; a game appearing on one feed and not another is a scheduling difference rather than a blackout.

Genuine blackouts are a narrower category — a game withheld in a specific market because another broadcaster holds the local rights. They are rare on national windows and more common on regional ones. ## The Connection Side, Briefly

Live sport is the hardest thing a home network does. Fast pans, crowd texture and ice detail all demand more bitrate than drama, and — crucially — a live stream's buffer is only seconds long. That means a **two-second dropout is visible as a stutter**, while a connection running at half the speed but perfectly steady will look fine.

Practical implications: prefer Ethernet or a 5 GHz connection for the main screen, expect evening congestion between roughly 7 p.m. and 11 p.m. when everyone in the neighbourhood is streaming, and remember that every stream runs **behind** the live event — commonly five to thirty seconds. That is why a neighbour's cheer or a phone notification can spoil a goal before you see it.
