Canadians have five days left to weigh in on the proposed **CRTC consumer protection code**, a single rulebook that would replace the four separate codes now governing internet, cellphone, home phone and television service. The deadline to file with the Commission is **August 11, 2026 at 8 p.m. Eastern time**, and a public hearing is scheduled to begin **November 30, 2026** in Gatineau, Quebec.

For households that have already left cable, the proceeding is worth reading even though it will not regulate them — because it is the clearest official statement yet of what Canada's regulator considers fair treatment in a television subscription.

![Canadian household reviewing internet and television service terms as the CRTC consumer protection code consultation closes](https://images.pexels.com/photos/12955517/pexels-photo-12955517.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## What the CRTC Consumer Protection Code Would Do

Broadcasting and Telecom Notice of Consultation **CRTC 2026-134**, released June 12, 2026, proposes consolidating four existing codes into one. The Commission says the goal is to "improve clarity and consistency of protections for consumers" while "reducing administrative burden for the industry."

The reasoning is straightforward: most Canadians now buy several of these services together, but their rights are written in four documents drafted up to fifteen years apart. A single CRTC consumer protection code would put them in one place.

> "Consumer protections should be simple. Through this consultation, we want to help Canadians understand their rights so they can make informed choices and better manage their bills." — Vicky Eatrides, Chairperson and Chief Executive Officer, CRTC

## Four Rulebooks, One Code

| Existing code | Year | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit and Disconnection Code | 2011 | Deposits, suspension and disconnection |
| Television Service Provider Code | 2016 | TV subscriptions from licensed providers |
| Wireless Code | 2017 | Mobile phone service |
| Internet Code | 2019 | Retail fixed internet from major providers |

Under the working document attached as Appendix 1 to the notice, the harmonized code would apply to retail television service for individual customers, home phone including VoIP, fixed internet access from major facilities-based providers, and mobile wireless — with full protections for postpaid service and a narrower set for prepaid.

The CRTC is explicit that Appendix 1 is a **discussion model**, not a decision. It is published to stimulate debate, and the Commission is asking specific questions about whether protections should extend to smaller internet providers, how "home phone services" should be defined, and how early cancellation fees should be reformed.

## What the CRTC Consumer Protection Code Proposes for TV Customers

The television-specific provisions in the working document are the part worth reading closely. They include:

- **Entry-level service disclosure** — availability and pricing of the basic package must be made clear.
- **Channel package transparency** — packages must be presented with their pricing details.
- **Programming change notice** — at least 30 days' notification before programming changes.
- **Individual channel changes** — customers must be able to modify their own channel selections.

None of this is law today. It is a proposal on the table, and interventions filed before August 11 form part of the record the Commission will weigh at the November hearing.

## Background: The Fee Ban That Left Television Out

The proposed CRTC consumer protection code follows a busy year for the Commission's Consumer Protections Action Plan. Three decisions have already been issued in 2026:

- **CRTC 2026-43** — bans providers from charging fees when customers cancel or change plans.
- **CRTC 2026-67** — strengthens notification when a plan or promotional discount is ending.
- **CRTC 2026-78** — requires improved self-service tools so customers can change or cancel plans themselves.

The important detail for TV subscribers is that 2026-43's fee ban amended the **Wireless Code and the Internet Code only**. Television service is governed by the separate Television Service Provider Code, and was not covered. That asymmetry — free to drop your internet plan, potentially not free to drop your TV package — is precisely the kind of inconsistency the harmonization proceeding is designed to address.

## Why the CRTC Consumer Protection Code Matters to Streaming Households

Here is the honest framing. The codes being merged bind **regulated Canadian telecommunications and television service providers**. An internet-delivered subscription service is not a licensed television service provider, so a harmonized code would not apply to it, and nothing in this proceeding changes that.

That does not make it irrelevant. Three reasons it is worth your attention:

1. **It is a published benchmark.** The working document is effectively the regulator's own checklist of fair terms — clear pricing, advance notice of changes, the ability to alter what you are paying for. You can hold any subscription against that standard, regulated or not.
2. **It shapes what you are leaving.** If you are weighing a move away from cable or satellite, the rules governing that contract may change. Anyone mid-decision should read our comparison of [IPTV and cable in Canada](/blog/iptv-vs-cable-canada) alongside the notice.
3. **Bills are moving now.** Rogers raised its StreamSaver bundle and certain internet plans on the same August date, a reminder that price changes arrive faster than regulation does.

Until a harmonized code exists, the protections you can actually rely on are the ones you arrange yourself — which is why the payment method you choose and the terms you save at checkout matter. Our guide to [IPTV payment methods in Canada](/blog/iptv-payment-methods-canada) covers that ground.

## How to Take Part Before August 11

The CRTC accepts submissions on the proposed CRTC consumer protection code from the public, not only from industry. Interventions can be filed:

- Through the CRTC's online intervention form
- By mail to the Secretary General, CRTC, Gatineau, Quebec K1A 0N2
- By fax to 819-994-0218
- Through the CRTC Conversations platform
- By sign language video, submitted via the online form

Submissions become part of the public record. The Commission has said it wants plain-language input from consumers about what confuses them on their bills.

## Key Takeaways

- The proposed CRTC consumer protection code would merge four codes dating from 2011 to 2019 into one.
- **Comments close August 11, 2026 at 8 p.m. ET**; the public hearing begins November 30, 2026 in Gatineau.
- The working document in Appendix 1 is a discussion model, not a decision, and includes TV-specific rules on entry-level pricing, package transparency, 30-day programming notice and channel changes.
- The 2026 fee ban under CRTC 2026-43 covers wireless and internet only — television was excluded, and this proceeding is where that gap gets examined.
- Internet-delivered services would not be bound by the code, but it remains a useful standard for judging any subscription.

## The Bottom Line

Nothing changes on a Canadian bill this week because of CRTC 2026-134, and a CRTC consumer protection code is still a proposal rather than a rule. What changes is the record: after August 11, the consumer side of the argument is fixed until the November hearing.

For cord-cutters, the practical value of the CRTC consumer protection code is as a yardstick. Read what the regulator thinks a fair television subscription looks like, then apply it to whatever you are actually paying for — and check our [FAQ](/faq) if you want to see how those terms translate in plain language.
