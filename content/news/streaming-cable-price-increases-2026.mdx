The **streaming price increases 2026** brought with them are impossible to ignore: over the past year, nearly every major service — Netflix, Prime Video, Disney+, Max, Paramount+, Peacock and ESPN — nudged its prices up, and cable bills are climbing right alongside them. For the first time, the "cheaper" alternative to cable is starting to feel expensive too. Here's an honest look at what's actually rising, why it's happening, and how to keep your monthly TV spend under control.

## Cable Bills Have Crossed $100 — and January Made It Worse

The old complaint about cable was that it kept getting pricier, and 2026 has done nothing to change that. The average cable bill now tops **$100 a month** in many regions, and in **January 2026** subscribers saw roughly another **$10–$20 per month** tacked on. That increase landed on top of the roughly **18% hike** Comcast's Xfinity put on its TV packages in 2025.

What makes cable especially frustrating is that the advertised price is almost never the price you pay. Broadcast-TV surcharges, regional-sports fees, equipment rental and "administrative" line items quietly inflate the bill month after month, so even a plan you never changed keeps getting more expensive. For a lot of households, that January statement was the final push toward cutting the cord.

## Streaming Price Increases 2026: Almost Every Service Went Up

The pitch for streaming was always that it was the affordable escape hatch from cable. That's getting harder to say with a straight face. Over the last 12 months, the list of services that raised prices or added new charges reads like the entire industry: **Netflix, Prime Video, Disney+, Max, Paramount+, Peacock and ESPN**. Prime Video introduced ads and then a fee to remove them; others simply raised the sticker price or pushed users toward pricier ad-free tiers.

Any single one of these increases looks trivial — a dollar here, two dollars there. But almost nobody subscribes to just one service anymore, and that's where the streaming price increases 2026 delivered really bite. When five separate bills each tick up at once, the combined jump is anything but small.

## Cord-Cutting Just Overtook Cable

The shift away from traditional pay-TV isn't a slow trickle anymore — it's a tipping point. Pay-TV penetration has fallen **below 50%**, and non-pay-TV households are projected to reach roughly **80.7 million by the end of the year**, surpassing the number of cable subscribers for the first time.

Price is the driving force, not a footnote. **More than 8 in 10 people** say cost is the number one reason they cut cable. And the pressure isn't easing: **nearly half** of the customers still on cable say they'd cancel if prices rise again — which, given the January increases, is exactly what happened. The direction of travel is clear, and it's away from the $100-plus monthly bill.

Sports fans feel this most sharply. Live games have long been the one thing keeping people tethered to cable, but rising regional-sports fees on one side and pricier live-TV streaming apps on the other have squeezed that group from both directions. When even watching your team costs more than it did last season, the case for rethinking the whole setup gets a lot stronger.

### The catch: "streaming stacking" recreates the cable bill

Here's the twist that's catching people off guard. To actually watch everything you want — a drama on one platform, a comedy on another, live sports somewhere else — most households now juggle **five or more apps**. Add a live-TV streaming service to get local channels and sports, and the monthly total quietly creeps back toward, or past, the cable bill you thought you'd escaped.

That fragmentation is the new cost. It's why some viewers are now doing something that would have sounded strange a year ago: **trimming their streaming subscriptions too**, cancelling one service to afford another. When keeping up with your favourite shows means managing five logins, five renewal dates and five separate price hikes, the "simple" streaming life stops feeling simple — or cheap.
