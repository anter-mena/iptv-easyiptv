The **TV cancellation rules Canada** applies are weaker than the ones covering wireless and internet service — a gap that matters more this month, after Statistics Canada reported **3.78 million non-employer businesses** in its 14 August release. That is 3.78 million households whose income arrives when clients pay rather than on a fixed payday, and for whom a recurring monthly charge is a different kind of commitment.

![TV cancellation rules Canada — a household reviewing television packages and monthly costs](https://images.pexels.com/photos/4474006/pexels-photo-4474006.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## TV Cancellation Rules Canada: What You Are Entitled To

- A provider **may not require 30 days' notice** to cancel television service
- You must receive **at least 30 days' notice** before a price change
- Any **early-cancellation charge must be in the written agreement** and the Critical Information Summary
- The June 2026 fee ban covers **wireless and internet only** — television is excluded
- A **pre-authorized debit can be revoked with 30 days' notice**, but that does not close the account

## The Gap Most People Get Wrong

Telecom Regulatory Policy **CRTC 2026-43** came into force on 12 June 2026 and banned providers from charging activation, modification and early-cancellation fees where no subsidised device is involved. It was widely reported, and widely misunderstood.

The ban applies under the **Wireless Code** and the **Internet Code**. Television service sits under a different instrument — the **Television Service Provider Code** — and was not included.

So the TV cancellation rules Canada households actually rely on are the older TVSP Code protections, which are real but narrower:

| Protection | Wireless / Internet | Television |
|---|---|---|
| Activation and modification fees banned | **Yes**, since 12 Jun 2026 | No |
| Early-cancellation fees banned | **Yes**, no subsidised device | No — must be disclosed instead |
| 30 days' notice cannot be required to cancel | Yes | **Yes** |
| Minimum 30 days' notice of price change | Yes | **Yes** |

The practical consequence is an ordering problem. If you are restructuring several services at once, the television line is the one where a charge can still legitimately appear — so read that agreement first, not last.

## Why Variable Income Changes the Calculation

Fixed monthly subscriptions are built on the same assumption as most consumer finance: that money arrives on known dates. For the growing number of Canadian households running a business with no payroll, that assumption does not hold.

Statistics Canada's June 2026 figures put non-employer businesses at 3.78 million, up from 3.59 million a year earlier, against 1.37 million employer businesses. Non-employer businesses now outnumber employers by roughly 2.8 to one.

For those households, subscriptions are usually the first thing reviewed in a slow month — they are discretionary, they renew silently, and cancelling one does not require anybody's permission. Knowing the TV cancellation rules Canada enforces turns that review from a guess into a decision.

## Background: A Code Under Review

The current split between four separate codes is itself under examination. In **Broadcasting and Telecom Notice of Consultation CRTC 2026-134**, released 12 June 2026, the Commission proposed merging the Deposit and Disconnection Code, the Television Service Provider Code, the Wireless Code and the Internet Code into a single Consumer Protection Code. A public hearing is scheduled for **30 November 2026** in Gatineau.

The working document accompanying that consultation raises television-specific proposals, including clearer entry-level service disclosure, channel-package pricing transparency and at least 30 days' notice of programming changes. Our earlier report on the [CRTC Consumer Protection Code](/news/crtc-consumer-protection-code) covers that proceeding in detail.

One point worth restating: these codes bind licensed Canadian television and telecom providers. Internet-delivered services are not covered by them, which makes the code a published benchmark for what a fair subscription looks like rather than a protection that automatically applies everywhere.

## Using the TV Cancellation Rules Canada Provides

The rules only help if the cancellation is executed properly. Five steps, in order:

1. **Read the Critical Information Summary first.** Providers must give you this short plain-language document summarising key terms, including any early-cancellation charge. It is faster than the full agreement and legally must match it.
2. **Cancel in writing, and date it.** Phone cancellations are valid but leave no record. Email or an in-account message creates one.
3. **Ask for the effective date in writing.** Since no provider may require 30 days' notice, confirm the account closes when you asked.
4. **Get a final bill confirmation.** Charges after the closing date are the most common dispute in this area.
5. **Cancel the payment method last, not first.** A pre-authorized debit can be revoked with 30 days' notice, but revoking it does not end the contract — it ends the payment, leaving the balance outstanding. For a personal PAD taken without proper authorisation, reimbursement can be claimed for up to 90 calendar days from the withdrawal, through your branch with a signed declaration.

## TV Cancellation Rules Canada: Key Takeaways

- The **June 2026 fee ban excludes television** — it covers the Wireless and Internet Codes only
- Providers **cannot require 30 days' notice** to cancel TV service
- You are owed **30 days' notice of any price increase**
- Early-cancellation charges must appear in the **agreement and Critical Information Summary**
- Revoking a pre-authorized debit **cancels the payment, not the contract**

## Conclusion

The TV cancellation rules Canada currently applies protect the two things that matter most — you can leave when you say you are leaving, and you must be told before the price moves. What they do not do is ban exit charges the way the newer wireless and internet rules do, and that asymmetry is easy to miss given how the June changes were reported.

With 3.78 million households now earning through a business rather than a payroll, the ability to review a recurring commitment quickly and cleanly has become a more common need than it once was. The rules support that; they just need to be used deliberately.
