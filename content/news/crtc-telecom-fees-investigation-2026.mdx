The record in the CRTC telecom fees investigation into Canada's three largest carriers **closed on August 10, 2026**, the deadline for reply submissions in a proceeding that could end with administrative monetary penalties of up to **$10 million per company**.

**In this article:** what closed today · the fees named · the rule behind it · what happens next · what it means for cord-cutters · key takeaways · sources

## What closed today in the CRTC telecom fees proceeding

**Telecom Notice of Consultation CRTC 2026-155**, released **June 30, 2026**, is a show-cause proceeding. It requires **Bell Canada**, **TELUS Communications Inc.** and **Rogers Communications Canada Inc.** to explain why certain fees they charge do not violate a prohibition the Commission put in place earlier this year.

| Stage | Date |
|---|---|
| Policy CRTC 2026-43 takes effect | **12 June 2026** |
| Notice of Consultation 2026-155 released | **30 June 2026** |
| Interventions due | **30 July 2026** |
| **Reply submissions due** | **10 August 2026** |
| Compliance deadline if an order issues | **60 days from the order** |

With the reply deadline passed, the CRTC now holds a complete record on the disputed CRTC telecom fees and a decision is pending. No outcome has been determined, and none of the companies has been found in violation.

## The CRTC telecom fees named in the notice

The Commission identified specific charges at each carrier:

| Company | Fee identified |
|---|---|
| **Bell Canada** | **$40 device-handling charge** on cellphone purchases made with wireless service |
| **TELUS** | **$15 fee** for a SIM card or eSIM |
| **Rogers** | **$40 device setup charge**; **$25 shipping fee** for online device orders; SIM fees |

Commission staff became aware of the Bell charge on **May 6, 2026** — less than a month before the prohibition took effect.

The CRTC's position, as set out in the notice, is that the companies must "show cause why these fees are not in violation" of the *Telecommunications Act*. The carriers' filings are their opportunity to argue that the charges fall outside the prohibition.

## The rule behind the CRTC telecom fees case

The prohibition comes from **Telecom Regulatory Policy CRTC 2026-43**, in force **12 June 2026**. It bans two things:

1. **Activation and modification fees** — defined as "any fee incurred as a result of activating a new retail telecommunications service plan or modifying an existing one", with exceptions for installation and optional services.
2. **Early cancellation fees** where no subsidised device has been provided.

Crucially, the policy defines its own scope:

- **Mobile wireless services** for individual and small business customers, across all wireless providers
- **Home internet services** for individual customers, from providers subject to the **Internet Code**

**Television services are not covered.** They fall under the separate Television Service Provider Code, and internet-delivered streaming services are not licensed television service providers at all.

That scope line is what makes the CRTC telecom fees proceeding relevant here, and it cuts in an unexpected direction.

## What it means for cord-cutters

A household that has dropped cable has not left the CRTC's consumer-protection perimeter. It has moved from the part that is **not** covered to the part that **is**.

| Service | Covered by the 2026-43 fee ban? |
|---|---|
| Cable or satellite TV subscription | **No** — Television Service Provider Code |
| Internet-delivered streaming service | **No** — not a licensed TV provider |
| **Home internet connection** | **Yes** |
| **Mobile wireless plan** | **Yes** |

For anyone who watches over the internet, **the connection is the regulated part of the bill.** Activation charges, plan-modification charges and — where no subsidised device is involved — early cancellation charges on a home internet account sit inside the prohibition the CRTC telecom fees proceeding is enforcing.

Two practical consequences follow.

**Check recent internet bills for setup or modification charges.** A fee applied on or after **12 June 2026** for activating or changing a home internet plan is the category the Commission is examining. Raising it with the provider costs nothing.

**Complaints go to the CCTS.** The Commission for Complaints for Telecom-television Services administers the consumer codes and handles disputes that a provider does not resolve directly.

## What happens next in the CRTC telecom fees case

The CRTC has not set a decision date. If it finds violations, the notice indicates it may impose:

- **Administrative monetary penalties** up to **$10,000,000 per company**
- Penalties up to **$25,000 per officer or director**
- **Mandatory compliance orders**, with **60 days** to comply from the date of the order

It is worth being precise about what has and has not happened. A show-cause proceeding is an opportunity for companies to justify a practice, not a finding against them. The closing of the reply period means the argument is complete — not that it has been decided.

The proceeding also sits inside a broader programme. The Commission's **Consumer Protections Action Plan** has produced a sequence of decisions through 2026, and a separate consultation is examining whether four existing codes — covering deposits and disconnection, television, wireless and internet — should be merged into a single **Consumer Protection Code**.

## Key takeaways

- The **CRTC telecom fees** show-cause record (**2026-155**) closed with reply submissions on **10 August 2026**.
- Fees named: **Bell $40 device handling**, **TELUS $15 SIM/eSIM**, **Rogers $40 setup + $25 shipping + SIM fees**.
- The underlying rule is **Policy CRTC 2026-43**, in force **12 June 2026**, banning activation and modification fees.
- Penalties can reach **$10,000,000 per company** and **$25,000 per officer or director**; compliance within **60 days** of any order.
- **Television is not covered** — but **home internet and mobile wireless are**, which is the part of a cord-cutter's bill that matters.
- No finding has been made against any company.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing in this proceeding for a cord-cutting household is the scope, not the penalty figure. Canada's fee protections stop at the TV subscription and start again at the connection — so the household that dropped cable is, on this specific point, better protected than the one that kept it.

Whether the three carriers' charges survive the CRTC's review is now for the Commission to decide. What is already settled is that activation and modification fees on home internet are prohibited, and that a bill showing one after 12 June 2026 is worth a phone call.
