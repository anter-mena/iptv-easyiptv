The **wholesale fibre rates Canada**'s telecom regulator finalised this spring are now under formal challenge. A coalition of eight independent internet providers, led by TekSavvy, has filed an application asking the CRTC to review **Telecom Order 2026-77**, arguing the approved rates are so high that competing on retail price is impossible. For anyone streaming television over the internet, this is the proceeding that sits underneath the bill.

*Reported August 2, 2026, based on the coalition's public filing announcement of late July 2026 and CRTC Telecom Order 2026-77. The CRTC has not yet ruled on the application. This is news reporting and general information.*

![Home internet setup affected by the wholesale fibre rates Canada regulators have approved](https://images.pexels.com/photos/31121857/pexels-photo-31121857.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## The 5 key facts

| # | Fact |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Telecom Order 2026-77** (April 24, 2026) set final cost-based rates for competitor access to incumbent fibre networks |
| 2 | It applies to **Bell Aliant, Bell Canada, Bell MTS, SaskTel and TELUS**, reaching up to **8.5 million households** |
| 3 | The order maintained a **30% markup** so incumbents recover fixed costs and retain investment incentives |
| 4 | Eight independent ISPs have applied to review it, citing a costing factor they say inflated rates by **25% to 30%** |
| 5 | The coalition also asks the CRTC to cut the markup from **30% to 15%** and correct speed-tier descriptions |

## What the coalition is arguing

The central claim about the **wholesale fibre rates Canada** approved in April is straightforward and, if accurate, decisive: the coalition says those rates are in some cases **higher than the retail prices the large carriers charge their own customers**. A reseller in that position can only sell at a loss or price above the incumbent — neither of which produces competition.

TekSavvy Vice-President **Andy Kaplan-Myrth** put it directly: *"Canadians were promised greater competition for fibre internet services, but these rates make competition impossible."*

The application seeks three specific remedies:

1. **Eliminate the costing factor** the coalition says artificially raised wholesale rates by 25% to 30%.
2. **Reduce the profit markup** on wholesale fibre access from 30% to 15%.
3. **Correct technical errors** in how speed tiers are described in the order.

The coalition comprises TekSavvy Solutions Inc., the BC Broadband Association, the Canada-Wide Internet Service Providers Association, Fibernetics Inc., ISP Telecom Inc., National Capital FreeNet Inc., Novus Entertainment Inc. and Purple Cow Internet Inc.

It is worth stating clearly what has and has not happened. The CRTC **has not ruled** on this application. The rates in Telecom Order 2026-77 remain in force. The incumbent carriers have consistently argued through this proceeding that wholesale rates must be high enough to preserve incentives to build fibre in the first place — a position the CRTC's 30% markup was designed to accommodate.

## Background: how the wholesale fibre rates Canada uses were set

The wholesale fibre framework is the product of a multi-year effort to open incumbent fibre-to-the-premises networks to competitors, on the theory that facilities-based competition alone was not delivering enough choice.

In April 2026 the CRTC finalised the rates, replacing interim rates that competitors had already been using to bring new offers to market. The Commission framed the decision as providing certainty for the industry while allowing competitors to keep offering choice. It set access rates that vary by carrier, region and speed tier — Bell Canada's fibre access rates, for example, were set in a range of roughly **$68.26 to $77.20** depending on tier — and applied retroactive adjustments dating to May 2024 for Bell Canada and TELUS in Quebec, and February 2025 for the other carriers.

The federal government separately upheld the CRTC's underlying ruling on wholesale internet access, keeping the policy direction intact. In other words, the principle of open access is settled; the argument now is entirely about the price, and the wholesale fibre rates Canada ends up with will determine whether the policy delivers what it promised.

## Why this matters for streaming households

Home internet is the one bill every streaming household pays regardless of which services it subscribes to. It is the floor under the entire cord-cutting calculation, which is why wholesale fibre rates Canada sets matter well beyond the telecom industry itself.

That makes the outcome here more consequential for streaming costs than most content-side news:

- **Cord-cutting maths depend on it.** Canadians have been leaving traditional TV in large numbers — households subscribing to traditional broadcast services have fallen by more than 1.2 million over five years. The savings from dropping cable are real only if the internet connection replacing it stays competitively priced.
- **Streaming stacking is already expensive.** With multiple subscriptions per household now common, internet price increases compound an existing squeeze rather than arriving in isolation.
- **Independent ISPs are the main source of price pressure.** If the wholesale fibre rates Canada has approved make undercutting impossible, the competitive discipline on incumbent retail pricing weakens.
- **The effect is slow but durable.** Wholesale fibre rates Canada sets now will shape retail offers for years, because network access pricing changes far less often than promotional retail pricing does.

One clarification worth making, because the two stories are often conflated: this proceeding has **nothing to do with IPTV site blocking**. Blocking orders are copyright matters decided by the courts and concern which services may be reached. This is an economic regulation proceeding about what competitors pay for network access. A licensed streaming service is unaffected by both.

## Key takeaways

- Eight independent ISPs have asked the CRTC to review **Telecom Order 2026-77** and the **wholesale fibre rates Canada** approved in April 2026.
- They seek removal of a costing factor they say inflated rates **25–30%**, and a markup cut from **30% to 15%**.
- The coalition argues the wholesale fibre rates Canada approved exceed incumbent retail prices, making competition unworkable.
- The CRTC **has not ruled**; current rates remain in force.
- The framework covers up to **8.5 million households** across five incumbent carriers.

## What to watch next

The next milestone is the CRTC's decision on whether to review Telecom Order 2026-77 at all — review applications must clear a threshold showing substantial doubt as to the correctness of the original decision before the Commission reconsiders the substance. Watch for responses from the incumbent carriers on the public record.

If the Commission does reopen the wholesale fibre rates Canada finalised in April, any revised rates would take months to work through to retail offers. Households should not expect a near-term change to what they pay for home internet, whichever way the decision goes.
